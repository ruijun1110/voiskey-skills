# Product Hunt Reply Library

This is a source of truth for Product Hunt reply structure, style, tone, and judgment. It is based on real maker replies found in Product Hunt threads, including replies from Willow Voice, Wispr Flow, and Typeless.

Use it as a reference. Adapt, combine, or reuse what fits the commenter and the moment. Do not force a pattern or mechanically rotate examples.

The examples are not Voiskey facts. Some contain product-specific claims from their original context. Never transfer those claims to a Voiskey reply. Retrieve all Voiskey facts only from the Brand System.

## What Strong Replies Tend to Do

- show that the maker read the exact comment
- answer direct questions before expanding
- explain a product decision when it helps
- match the comment's seriousness and length
- let the product’s underlying purpose make the difference clear
- treat feedback as information rather than an objection
- be precise when trust, limitations, or availability are involved

## Common Failure Modes

- replying only to praise while leaving a real question unanswered
- opening every reply with generic thanks or automatic praise
- turning a one-line comment into a feature list
- treating a concern as hostility
- making a casual roadmap commitment
- asking a broad question that creates no useful signal
- using the same cadence or sentence shape repeatedly

## Recognition and Appreciation

Use when the commenter shares praise, encouragement, an early experience, or a personal use case. Be warm and specific when there is something concrete to reflect. Keep it short when there is not.

Watch out for: treating every positive comment as an opportunity for a product pitch or ignoring the specific detail the commenter shared.

Examples:

> “Thanks so much, [name]. Means the world to us. The team is super happy to hear this.”

> “Woohoo! Thanks for being one of our most active customers.”

> “That’s so nice to hear. Thank you so much for being a beta tester. Let me know what you think as you keep using it.”

> “Glad to hear you’ve been enjoying the product!”

> “Thanks so much for sharing your experience. Really appreciate you chiming in.”

## Direct Answer and Practical Detail

Use only when the commenter asks for a direct fact about a capability, availability, price, or practical behavior. Give the answer first and add the minimum detail that makes it useful. Do not use this section for questions about what Voiskey is, why it matters, what makes it special, or how it compares.

Watch out for: beginning with background, adding unrelated features, or answering a different question than the one asked.

Examples:

> “Yep, we’ve optimized it to write in your style, get names right, and make sure you have to edit as rarely as possible.”

> “It works well in noisy environments, yes. We also do some filtering for background noise.”

> “We don't support formatting for now. The idea is to make it simple and not worry about format. If there’s a strong need, I’d love to hear your thoughts.”

> “For now we support a simple embed code generation, but I’d really like to hear more about the use cases you have in mind.”

> “For now we don't support bringing your own key. We’re focused on making it easy for beginners to get started with zero local setup.”

## Product Story and Perspective

Use when the commenter wants to understand Voiskey itself, including why it exists, what makes it matter, how it compares, or whether it fits their work. Find the part of the Voiskey story that answers the question and share it from our own perspective. The comment tells us what to address. It does not decide how we tell the story.

Watch out for: naming a Brand System concept without making its meaning felt, restating product copy, adding facts to make the reply feel complete, listing capabilities, or defining Voiskey through another product.

Examples:

> “Classic PRDs are kind of a relic. But that’s exactly what we’re trying to change.”

> “Most software just adds another tab to manage. We wanted to remove work, not add it.”

> “I built it because I needed it for my own design work.”

> “Fair concern, and honestly the reason we built it the way we did.”

> “This is the reason why something like this was not possible before AI. It gave me the spark to build it.”

## Invitation to Try

Use when trying Voiskey would help the commenter more than another explanation. Present the offer as a welcome from the makers. The Brand System supplies the exact offer and terms.

Watch out for: treating the offer as a plan detail, adding it to a reply that is already complete, or using it as a generic closing line.

Examples:

> “Would love for you to try it. Your mix sounds like a perfect test case.”

> “Best way to see it in action is to try it yourself. There are 5 free projects included, so you can generate real carousels right after downloading.”

> “Have you had a chance to try it? I’d love to know if the experience matches what you imagined.”

> “Bookshelf was built to solve that. I’d love for you to give it a try and tell me how it works for you.”

> “If you’d like to try it out for free, I’ll send you a free use code. All we ask in return is honest feedback on the experience.”

## Value, Fit, and Price Hesitation

Use when someone is unsure whether Voiskey will be worth paying for. Address the concern directly with the relevant benefit, limit, or plan detail. If firsthand use would help more, use Invitation to Try.

Watch out for: answering with a paid-plan feature list, letting another product define the conversation, or pushing an upgrade before the commenter has experienced the value.

Examples:

> “We have a free tier to get started, and then our pricing is based on paid customers that we are managing.”

> “Start with the first half only. If it earns its place, you can decide about the second half later with actual information instead of a guess.”

> “Unused credits stay usable. Buy when you need, use whenever, no pressure.”

> “What we could do is postpone your free trial if you like?”

> “We sure do have a free trial, with a two-week Pro trial when you upgrade.”

## Feedback, Requests, and Problems

Use when someone reports a bug, highlights a limitation, proposes an improvement, or describes a workflow the product does not yet cover. Acknowledge the friction directly. State only what is known. Ask for details only when they will help identify the underlying need or reproduce the issue.

Watch out for: promising a roadmap item, guessing at the root cause, arguing with feedback, or using generic language such as “we are listening.”

Examples:

> “Thanks for flagging this. This is fixed now!”

> “Could you share the steps you followed and which browser or device you were on?”

> “Quick update: it was a region-related issue so only some users experienced it.”

> “That one’s on us. We’re working on a proper fix.”

> “Good feedback! Do you have any suggestions for other electrical ones?”

## Conversation and Learning

Use when the reply can turn a real user observation into useful insight. Ask a focused question only when the answer would clarify a concrete use case, preference, or tradeoff.

Watch out for: asking for feedback by default, asking broad questions that generate no signal, or creating an unnecessary exchange after a complete answer.

Examples:

> “What has been your number one use case?”

> “What languages would you use with [product]?”

> “Try it out and let me know how it works for you. Would love your feedback.”

> “What would make the most sense for your target audience from your perspective?”

> “What sizes are you usually using?”

## Length and Format

Use one sentence for social comments. Use two to four sentences for most questions, feedback, and questions about differentiation. Use two short paragraphs only when the comment involves trust, privacy, accessibility, a substantial question about differentiation, or a detailed personal story.

Use a name or handle when it sounds natural. Keep paragraphs short. Use plain text unless the conversation calls for formatting.

## Final Reading Test

- Does the opening prove that the comment was read?
- Does the reply answer the question before expanding?
- Does the response add only what helps this commenter?
- Is any follow-up question useful and specific?
- Does the reply sound human if read alongside several other replies from the same maker?
